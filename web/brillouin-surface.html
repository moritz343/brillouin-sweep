Floquet–Bloch · 3D mass-in-mass lattice · 0 ≤ kx, ky, kz ≤ π/a

Dispersion over the faces of the 3D irreducible Brillouin zone

Each cell is a host mass with a resonator inside it. The resonator hangs on three springs rx, ry, rz, so it has its own frequency in each direction. The host connects to its six neighbours through springs kx, ky, kz. The irreducible zone is the box 0 ≤ ki ≤ π/a. The usual band diagram walks its 12 edges, and this page also plots ω over its 6 faces, unfolded flat like a cube net.

Shear ratio

A real spring or beam also resists sideways motion a little. Here each host bond gets sideways stiffness s × its axial stiffness. At s = 0, motion along x only feels the x-springs, so each direction becomes an independent 1D chain and the surfaces go flat.

Diagonal coupling

Springs g from each host to its 12 diagonal neighbours ⟨110⟩. A diagonal spring pulls along two axes at once, so it mixes the directions of motion. For example, the x–y coupling is proportional to sin kxa · sin kya. On every face, motion perpendicular to the face stays separate (mirror symmetry), so its crossings stay exact. The two in-plane motions mix, so their crossings open up inside the face.

What this lattice shows

  • Three resonances: on a fixed host, the resonator would ring at √(rx/μ), √(ry/μ) and √(rz/μ). Each one opens a gap for waves whose motion is along that axis, and a complete gap exists only where all three overlap.
  • Anisotropy: the surfaces show how the slope (group velocity) changes with direction across every face, which the line plot only shows along the 12 edges.
  • Crossings and avoided crossings: without coupling (g = 0), sheets for different directions of motion pass through each other. With diagonal springs, crossings between the two in-plane motions on a face open into avoided crossings, and the colour blends smoothly from one motion to the other. Crossings with the out-of-plane motion stay exact because the face is a mirror plane.
  • Where the band edges sit: in all the examples here, the complete gap opens between a corner (R) and Γ, so the path, the faces and the volume agree. The surface view earns its keep on the crossings, not on the gap.

Model

Each mass moves in 3D. Axial host bonds along i have stretching stiffness ki and sideways stiffness s·ki. Optional central springs g to the 12 ⟨110⟩ neighbours add 2g(1 − cos k·d) n nᵀ for each bond direction n. The resonator hangs on rx, ry, rz (springs on both sides combined), with host mass 1 and resonator mass μ. At each k, the page solves the 6 × 6 mass-weighted dynamical matrix numerically (Jacobi method) and takes each mode's share of motion along x, y and z from its eigenvector. Frequencies are in √(k/m₁) units and k is in π/a.

Background and state of the art

How dispersion is normally shown. For a periodic medium, the Floquet–Bloch dispersion ω(k) is a set of sheets over the Brillouin zone. In practice it is almost always drawn along the edges of the irreducible Brillouin zone (IBZ). For 3D crystals, the k-paths are standardised for every Bravais lattice (Setyawan & Curtarolo, Comput. Mater. Sci. 2010). The usual justification is that band extrema tend to lie on the IBZ boundary. Over 2D zones, full surfaces are common, for example Dirac cones and isofrequency contours used for negative refraction and beam steering.

Is the path enough? This assumption is known to fail. Harrison, Kuchment, Sobolev & Winn (J. Phys. A 40, 2007) constructed periodic operators whose spectral edges lie inside the zone. Craster, Antonakakis, Makwana & Guenneau (Phys. Rev. B 86, 2012) warned of the "dangers of using the edges of the Brillouin zone". Maurin, Claeys, Deckers & Desmet (Int. J. Solids Struct. 135, 2018) ran hundreds of porous phononic-crystal simulations across the 17 plane crystallographic groups. They found the IBZ contour reliable only for highly symmetric lattices, and that a gap seen on the contour can be absent in the full zone. These systematic studies are all in 2D. I did not find a comparable statistical study for 3D lattices.

Dispersion surfaces in 3D. Because ω(kx, ky, kz) cannot be drawn directly, 3D studies use high-symmetry paths, isofrequency surfaces, or selected planes. The closest example I found is a study of accordion-like 3D metamaterials (arXiv:1804.02188). It plots dispersion surfaces on chosen cross-sections of the 3D IBZ to check which band gaps survive at oblique incidence. I did not find the specific construction used here: all faces of the 3D IBZ unfolded into one net, with the standard path drawn on top and the modes coloured by their direction of motion.

Direction-dependent resonators. The unit cell here relates to 3D anisotropic locally resonant metamaterials. Ma et al. (Nat. Commun. 2016) built a solid metamaterial whose resonant unit has three translational modes and showed "polarization band gaps", where each direction of motion can be suppressed selectively. Faraci, Mendicino, Vincenti & Comi (Appl. Sci. 2023) describe such materials with an anisotropic, frequency-dependent effective mass tensor that predicts polarization band gaps and polarization conversion.

Crossings and symmetry. The colour behaviour on the net reflects a well-known mechanism. Bands of opposite mirror parity cannot mix, so their crossings are protected (He & Chan, arXiv:1409.3939, for photonic crystals). In phononic crystals, mirror-protected nodal rings with drumhead surface states have been observed experimentally (Deng et al., Nat. Commun. 10, 1769, 2019). Such degeneracies usually lie on mirror planes, which for an orthorhombic lattice are exactly the faces of the IBZ. That makes the face net a natural place to look for them.

About this project

During my PhD in metamaterials, almost everyone around me worked on 2D periodic structures. The routine was always the same: apply Floquet–Bloch boundary conditions to the unit cell, sweep the wavevector along the edge of the irreducible Brillouin zone, and draw the band diagram along that 1D line. At some point I asked myself what the same idea looks like one dimension up. For a 3D material, the irreducible zone is a solid, its boundary is a surface, and so the natural counterpart of the band diagram should be a dispersion surface drawn over that boundary.

This page is my attempt to make that concrete. I take a 3D mass-in-mass lattice: a host mass coupled to its neighbours by springs kx, ky, kz, and a resonator inside it held by its own springs rx, ry, rz, so it resonates at a different frequency in each direction. For this lattice the irreducible zone is a box. Its 12 edges are the usual band-diagram path, and its 6 faces unfold into a cube net, over which I plot every band as a sheet. I colour each sheet by the direction in which the masses move, and add a diagonal coupling to show how crossing sheets turn into avoided crossings.

Two things came out of it. First, for band-gap work the extra dimension mostly confirms the standard path, at least for this kind of lattice: the gap edges sit at the corners of the zone. Second, the surface view shows something the line plot cannot. You see where bands of different polarization cross, which of those crossings survive coupling, and how they line up with the mirror planes of the lattice. The same procedure works for any FEM unit-cell model: sweep the Bloch wavevector over the faces of the IBZ instead of its edges, at roughly four to ten times the cost of a standard band diagram.
